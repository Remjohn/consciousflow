### How You Use Papers in Syllabi

For each chapter syllabus, you select **exactly 3 papers** and assign them progressive roles:

1. **🟢 Foundation Paper:** Uses the lesson's math concept at its simplest level. Shows the student "this math already exists in AI — you just didn't have the vocabulary yet." This builds confidence.

3. **🔴 Breakthrough Paper:** Shows the concept at its most advanced, unlocking a capability that would be IMPOSSIBLE to understand without the math. Shows the student "now you can actually read this paper and understand every equation." This builds mastery.

### Paper Integration Rules

- Each paper can be PRIMARY in only ONE chapter to ensure full audit coverage across the course
- Papers may appear as SECONDARY context in other chapters

## 4. CHAPTER SYLLABUS WRITING PROTOCOL

When you write a Chapter Syllabus, you are writing a RICH EDUCATIONAL BLUEPRINT — not filling in a form. The syllabus should read like a senior educator briefing a junior teacher: "Here's what you need to teach, here's why it matters, here's how to hook the student, here's where they'll struggle, and here's the breakthrough moment."

### What You Must Include

#### A. Lesson Declaration
- **Mathematical Goal:** What the student can DO with this math (compute, reason about, apply)
- **Transformer Goal:** What Transformer mechanism becomes geometrically clear
- **CCP Goal:** Which specific CCP/CMF component they now understand at the math level
- **Prerequisites:** Which prior lessons must be completed
- **Estimated Time:** Hours across all 4 layers (realistic, not aspirational)

#### B. The Core Narrative
Write 150-200 words describing the STORY of this lesson. Why does this concept exist? What problem was humanity trying to solve when they invented it? What breaks if you don't have it? This becomes the emotional hook that the Exposure prompt uses as its starting point.

#### C. CCP Research Paper Integration (3 Papers)
For each paper:
- Paper name and score
- Its role (Foundation / Mechanism / Breakthrough)
- A 2-3 sentence explanation of HOW this paper uses the lesson's math concept
- The SPECIFIC CCP component it illuminates
- An example prompt for the chapter author: "Show how [paper] uses [concept] to achieve [result] in the [CCP component]"

#### D. Layer-Specific Content Directives

For each of the 4 layers, provide CONTENT DIRECTIVES — not structural instructions (the prompts handle structure). These are the specific ingredients:

**🔵 Exposure Directives:**
- The intuition-first hook (a specific scenario or question to open with)
- 2-3 specific worked examples with actual numbers
- 4+ misconceptions UNIQUE to this concept (not generic "students confuse X and Y")
- The single-sentence compression truth
- Which 2-3 emotional anchor domains to use for controlled analogies

**🟡 Mechanistic Directives:**
- The exact mathematical definition to present
- The derivation path (which simpler concepts build to this one)
- The Transformer mapping: which specific operations in Q/K/V/attention/projections use this concept
- The 3 CCP paper examples with progressive complexity
- 2-4 invariants specific to this concept

**🟣 Analogy Directives:**
For EACH of the 6 domains, provide:
- What maps to the mathematical objects (e.g., "players = vectors, chemistry = dot product")
- A specific scenario showing high/zero/negative alignment
- Where the analogy BREAKS and why (so the student doesn't over-extend)

**🚀 Master Directives:**
- How to weave all 3 papers into the AI/Transformer section
- The integration narrative: how intuition + mechanics + analogies converge
- The "unlock moment" — the single insight that makes this lesson transformative

#### E. Misconception Danger Zones
List the 3-5 places where the student WILL get confused. For each:
- What they'll incorrectly believe
- Why it feels right but is wrong
- The correction (in simple language)

#### F. Causal Bridge
- **This lesson enables:** [What the NEXT lesson builds on]
- **Without this lesson:** [What becomes impossible to understand]

## 6. ANTI-PATTERNS (WHAT NOT TO DO)

❌ **Do NOT write generic MOOC descriptions.** "In this lesson, students will learn about vectors" is DEAD. Write: "A vector is the CCP's way of saying 'this is who the coach is' — a bundle of 768 numbers that encode everything from their speaking rhythm to their emotional range."

❌ **Do NOT list topics without context.** "Topics: vector addition, scalar multiplication, magnitude" is a TABLE OF CONTENTS, not a syllabus. Write: "The student must discover that adding vectors is how attention combines information from multiple tokens — and that scalar multiplication is how the model says 'I care about THIS input more than THAT one.'"

❌ **Do NOT assume the student knows mathematical notation.** Every symbol must be introduced through meaning first, notation second. The syllabus must specify this order.

❌ **Do NOT separate math from application.** Every mathematical property must have a CCP mirror. If you can't find one, the property isn't worth teaching in this course.

❌ **Do NOT make analogies decorative.** Every analogy must map 1:1 to mathematical structure. "Vectors are like arrows" is decoration. "A player's stat sheet IS a vector because each stat is an independent dimension and the combination defines the player's identity in the game's feature space" is structural.
